orbit-launcher · design presentation v2 · the orbital system

The universe holds still. You drift.

v1 shipped the bones: starfield, wordmark, five choices. v2 fixes the physics and gives the menu a home. Three laws govern every screen from here on — everything else in this document follows from them.

1 · Stars are fixed points Real stars don't wander independently. The field is rigid — it never scrolls or drifts. It rotates as one body, slowly, because you are turning in space. Nearer stars sweep marginally faster than distant ones; that parallax is the only depth cue. Twinkle is the one thing a star does on its own.
2 · All motion is your motion Launch is an arrival: you fly through the field into the centre and decelerate. Entering a flow flies you closer; esc pulls you back. The idle rotation is you drifting. Motion always means where you are — never decoration.
3 · The menu is a place A single large sphere holds the wordmark and menu — a body you're in orbit of. Smaller planets circle it on their own paths and periods, and genuinely pass behind it. Text renders above everything, without exception.

01 Arrival — flying in

F1 · TravelStars streak radially outward from the vanishing point — the view through a windshield at speed. Longest streaks nearest the edges.
F2 · DecelerationStreaks shorten over ~600ms as you brake. Outer stars settle into fixed points first, centre last.
F3 · The body resolvesOnce the field is still, the sphere's limb draws on — a sweep around its circumference (~400ms), like something coming into focus as you settle in front of it.

F4 · At restWordmark and menu fade in on the sphere; planets are already in motion as they appear — the system was always here, you just arrived at it.
  • Total arrival budget: under 1.5 seconds, and any key skips straight to F4. The sequence sells the metaphor once; it must never cost a returning user time.
  • Terminal implementation — streaks are 2–4 cell runs of · ∙ ● along precomputed radial lines, advancing on the existing tick. Deceleration is just shortening the run length per frame. The limb sweep lights arc cells progressively clockwise.
  • ORBIT_LAUNCHER_NO_ANIMATION=1 (and dumb terminals) jump directly to F4, fully rendered and static — the same rule the starfield already follows today.

Four bodies, four temperaments

  • Speeds are roughly a quarter of the last pass — periods now run 40s (the little ember moon, closest in) to 150s (the pale outer wanderer). Watch for a few seconds and it reads as calm drift; look away and back and the sky has genuinely rearranged.
  • The centre is a sphere, not a ring. It's opaque, so a planet crossing its far side is fully hidden and re-emerges on the other limb — the thing that was missing before. A static off-centre inner shadow gives it a terminator so it reads as lit, not as a hole cut in the starfield.
  • The starfield rotates as one rigid body — you are turning, slowly, roughly one revolution every six minutes. Two layers at slightly different rates give real parallax. No star ever moves relative to its neighbours.
  • Text is optically centred now — the wordmark compensates for its own trailing letter-space, and each menu row centres its label between an equal-width caret and spacer, so labels never shift as you move the selection.
  • Bottom bar is just the version, right-aligned, in the old footer's weight and colour. The keybind hints are gone.

03 Layering — who's allowed on top

Text — wordmark, menu, version, statusz5 · always wins
Planets, near half of orbit — lower half of each pathz3
The central sphere — static, opaque, occludingz2
Planets, far half of orbit — upper half, hidden behind the spherez1
Starfield — rigid, rotating, twinklingz0
  • Depth from one rule: a planet is "far" on the upper half of its ellipse and "near" on the lower half — how a tilted orbit viewed edge-on actually works. z-order snaps at the two crossings; no smooth z needed.
  • In the terminal this is compositor order, drawn back to front into the cell buffer each frame: stars → far planets → sphere cells → near planets → text. The sphere pass writes opaque cells (spaces included), which is exactly what makes occlusion work — the far planets simply never get drawn there.
  • Text always wins literally: the text pass is last and opaque, so a planet can never bleed through a menu label mid-crossing.

Same space, closer in

  • Yes — flow screens keep a twinkling starfield, and it keeps rotating on the same clock as the splash. It's deliberately sparser and dimmer (about a third the density, roughly half the brightness) so it never competes with reading, but it's the same sky, still turning. You didn't go somewhere else; you moved closer.
  • Selecting a menu item is travel: the camera pushes forward ~300ms — stars stretch briefly, the sphere swells past the viewport edge — and you arrive. esc reverses the identical move. Navigation and motion are the same thing, so the spatial model teaches itself.
  • The sphere never fully leaves. Its limb survives as a huge faint arc through a corner — you're close to it now, not elsewhere. This replaces v1's hard cut from atmospheric splash to flat form.
  • Every flow screen is centred, fixing the current build's real inconsistency where the splash centres but Install / Update / Remove / Repair hug the top-left on fixed padding.
  • Planets don't follow you in. Flow screens are for decisions — the residents here are the corner arc and the quiet field. Data entry keeps v1's rule: atmosphere yields to focus.

05 Motion vocabulary — the complete set

  • Rotation — the whole starfield, everywhere, always. One revolution per ~6 minutes; distant layer ~8.5. This is you drifting.
  • Twinkle — stars only; slow 5–12s cycles, never more than a third of the field mid-twinkle at once. The idle heartbeat.
  • Orbit — planets, main menu only; constant angular speed per planet, periods 40–150s. Never paused, never accelerated: the system persists rather than reacting to you.
  • Travel — radial streak plus sphere scale; arrival and flow enter/exit only. ≤1.5s arrival, ~300ms transitions, always skippable, always symmetric (enter forward = esc backward).
  • Nothing else moves. Any new animation is one of these four or it doesn't ship.

06 Suggestions — four, chosen for restraint

1 · The sphere shows whether Orbit is actually running

orbit-launcher already detects an existing deployment on startup — it has to, to decide between Install and Update. Right now that knowledge only shows up as which menu item is preselected. Let the body itself carry it: dormant when nothing is installed (unlit limb, muted outline, no glow) and lit when Orbit is up and healthy (bright limb, faint green cast). One glance, before reading a single word, tells you the state of your server.

Cost: low — the detection already exists; this is a palette swap on cells you're drawing anyway.

2 · Arrival doubles as the completion moment

Install now hands the terminal to install.sh, so its scrolling output is the real progress display and orbit-launcher takes the screen back at the end. That handback is the strongest beat in the whole app and currently it's just a text screen. Make it an arrival in reverse: the field settles, the sphere lights (suggestion 1's transition, played once, ~500ms), and the URL lands as the hero line beneath it. You watched a wall of log output; now the planet is alive.

Cost: low — reuses the arrival compositor and the lit/dormant states; no new machinery.

3 · One honest line where the keybind hints used to be

Dropping the footer text reclaims the bottom of the screen, and there's one thing more useful there than ↑↓ navigate: a quiet status line, only when there's something true to say — a detected deployment's address, or a waiting launcher update. Left-aligned, version on the right, same faint weight. Silence when there's nothing worth saying, which is most of the time.

Cost: low — the update check and deployment detection both already run at startup.

4 · The system scales down instead of breaking

At 80×24 a 340px-equivalent sphere doesn't fit. Rather than clipping, define three tiers: full (sphere, four planets, both star layers), compact (smaller sphere, two planets, one star layer), and minimal (no sphere, just centred text on a sparse field) below roughly 60×18. Same layout logic, fewer bodies — so a cramped SSH window degrades gracefully instead of rendering a broken circle.

Cost: medium — needs real terminal-size testing, but it's the difference between "looks great on my machine" and "looks right everywhere".

  • Deliberately not suggested: planets reacting to selection, sound, colour-shifting on hover, animated menu transitions, a spinning sphere surface. All of them break law 2 — motion would stop meaning "where you are" and start meaning "look at me".
